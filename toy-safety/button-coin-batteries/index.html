Button and coin batteries

What Reese’s Law and Health Canada say to read on a button or coin battery compartment, and where to search a recall.

Button batteriesMagnetsWater beadsRide-ons and scooters

What to read on the label

  • On a product with a replaceable button cell or coin battery, read the compartment. CPSC button-cell guidance says ANSI/UL 4200A-2023, codified at 16 CFR part 1263, requires a tool or at least two independent and simultaneous hand movements to open, and says the batteries must not come free in use and abuse testing.
  • The same CPSC page says 16 CFR part 1263 does not apply to a toy for children under 14 if that toy meets the battery accessibility and labeling requirements of 16 CFR part 1250, which incorporates ASTM F963. It says section 4.25 of the toy standard covers battery-operated toys. The September 11, 2023 CPSC release says toys with button cell or coin batteries are required to have a secure closure that needs a screwdriver, coin, or tool to open.
  • Health Canada toy safety tips say to look for a secure, child-resistant battery compartment that needs a tool or multiple steps to open, and to be especially careful with button batteries.

How to search a recall

For a purchase in the United States, search the brand and the model on CPSC Recalls. For a purchase in Canada, search the same words on Health Canada recalls and safety alerts. Open the notice and match the model number on the unit. A family name on the box can point at a different model.

Read the model number on the toy and on the battery door. A book, a watch, and a remote can all use a coin cell and still be different products. Match the number on the unit to the number on the notice.

Patterns in official notices

Each pattern below is one official page. The sentence is what that page says.

  • CPSC: Button Cell and Coin Battery Business Guidance. The page says Reese’s Law (P.L. 117-171) requires the rule in 16 CFR part 1263, which incorporates ANSI/UL 4200A-2023. It says the performance and labeling requirements apply to products manufactured or imported on or after October 23, 2023, with enforcement discretion so they apply to products manufactured or imported after March 19, 2024. It says a button cell or coin battery subject to part 1263 is a single cell battery with a diameter greater than its height, and that zinc-air button cells are excluded from part 1263.
  • CPSC: Reese’s Law leads to a federal safety standard. The September 11, 2023 release says Reese’s Law was enacted August 16, 2022. It says these batteries can burn through a child’s throat or esophagus in as little as two hours if swallowed. It says that from 2011 through 2021 CPSC was aware of 27 deaths and an estimated 54,300 emergency-room injuries associated with ingested or inserted button cell or coin batteries. It says to call the National Battery Ingestion Hotline at 800-498-8666 or Poison Help at 800-222-1222, and to take a child who may have swallowed one to the nearest emergency department.
  • Health Canada: Button batteries pose life-threatening dangers to young children. The advisory says a swallowed button battery can lodge in the throat, where saliva starts an electrical current and a chemical burn that can burn through the esophagus, windpipe, and main artery in as little as two hours. It says depleted batteries can still cause serious injury. It says to check that compartments stay secure, to look for compartments that are not easy to open, and to keep batteries out of reach, especially for children under five. It says CHIRPP received reports of 125 button-battery injuries in 2020.
  • Health Canada: Toy safety tips: battery compartments. The page says to look for toys with secure, child-resistant battery compartments that require a tool or multiple steps to open, and says button batteries are very dangerous if swallowed. It also says to search the recalls and safety alerts database before you buy.

Questions

What should I see on a button-battery door?

CPSC guidance for 16 CFR part 1263 says a replaceable button cell or coin battery compartment needs a tool or at least two independent and simultaneous hand movements to open. The September 11, 2023 CPSC release says toys with these batteries need a secure closure that takes a screwdriver, coin, or tool. Health Canada says to look for a compartment that needs a tool or multiple steps.

Does Reese’s Law skip every toy?

CPSC says 16 CFR part 1263 does not apply to a toy for children under 14 only if that toy meets the battery accessibility and labeling requirements of 16 CFR part 1250. The CPSC release still says those toys need a secure closure that takes a screwdriver, coin, or tool.

Where do I search this product?

Search the brand and the model on CPSC Recalls for a US purchase and on Health Canada recalls and safety alerts for a Canadian purchase. Match the number on the toy to the number on the notice.
