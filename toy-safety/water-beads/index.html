Water beads

What the current CPSC water-bead rule and the Health Canada advisory say, including which products the US rule covers.

Button batteriesMagnetsWater beadsRide-ons and scooters

What to read on the label

  • Read the date and the warnings on the package. CPSC water-bead guidance says the Safety Standard for Toys: Requirements for Water Beads, 16 CFR 1250.4, applies to water bead toy products effective March 12, 2026. It defines a water bead as a liquid-absorbent polymer that expands when soaked.
  • The March 12, 2026 CPSC release says water beads manufactured after March 12, 2026 must meet the performance, labeling, and testing requirements. It also says some water bead products are not marketed as children’s toys and fall outside the rule, and to keep those out of spaces where children live or play.
  • Health Canada’s water-bead advisory says caregivers of children under five should avoid having water beads in the household or classroom, even when they were bought for an older child or an adult.

How to search a recall

For a purchase in the United States, search the brand and the model on CPSC Recalls. For a purchase in Canada, search the same words on Health Canada recalls and safety alerts. Open the notice and match the model number on the unit. A family name on the box can point at a different model.

Search the brand and the kit name. A sensory kit, a stress ball, a vase filler, and a gardening product can all contain water beads. Read whether the listing calls the product a toy.

Patterns in official notices

Each pattern below is one official page. The sentence is what that page says.

  • CPSC: Water Bead Toys Business Guidance. The page says the final rule, 90 Fed. Reg. 57,820, is 16 CFR 1250.4. It says water beads that fit in the small parts cylinder when dry must pass through a 5.0 mm funnel or sieve once fully expanded. It says water beads in toys shall not have more than 325 micrograms of acrylamide extractable from 100 small beads or one large bead. It says the toy, the packaging, and the container must carry the warning label.
  • CPSC: The water-bead standard takes effect. The March 12, 2026 release says water beads manufactured after that date must meet the new requirements, and that water beads that fail the standard are illegal to sell in the United States. It says water beads can grow up to 100 times their original size when exposed to water. It says CPSC data show an estimated 6,300 water-bead ingestion injuries treated in US emergency departments from 2017 to 2022, and at least one reported death, a 10-month-old girl in 2023. It says to remove water beads from any area where young children may be present.
  • Health Canada: Water beads may pose life-threatening risks to young children. The advisory, updated February 16, 2024, says these beads can grow up to 1,500 times their size in water and can keep growing inside the body, causing intestinal or bowel obstruction. It says beads in the nose or ear need medical care. It recommends that caregivers of children under five avoid having water beads at home or in a classroom, and that children five and up be closely supervised if beads are used.
  • Health Canada: Water beads on the toy-type page. The page says not to allow children under 5 years old to play with water beads, to clean spills right away, and to get medical help if a child may have swallowed them. It says not to wait for symptoms.

Questions

Are water beads banned?

CPSC says water bead toys manufactured after March 12, 2026 must meet 16 CFR 1250.4, and that water beads that fail that standard are illegal to sell in the United States. The same release says products that are not marketed as children’s toys fall outside the rule. Health Canada’s advisory tells caregivers of children under five to avoid having water beads in the home or classroom. That advisory is not written as a ban of every bead.

Why do the size descriptions differ?

The March 12, 2026 CPSC release says water beads can grow up to 100 times their original size. The Health Canada advisory says they can grow up to 1,500 times their size. Each sentence is quoted from that agency’s page.

What should I do if a child may have swallowed one?

Health Canada says to call your provincial or territorial Telehealth number if you suspect ingestion, and to seek medical care for a bead in the nose or ear. The page lists symptoms that include vomiting, abdominal pain, swelling, constipation, lethargy, drooling, trouble breathing or swallowing, and loss of appetite. It says not to wait for symptoms.
