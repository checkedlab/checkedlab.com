Kitchen fires

CPSC, NFPA, and USFA guidance for Thanksgiving cooking fires and fire extinguishers, plus the CPSC notice for Kidde plastic-handle extinguishers.

Turkey fryersPressure cookersTurkey temperaturesKitchen fires

What to read on the label

  • The November 19, 2025 CPSC holiday cooking release says 45 percent of annual residential fires are cooking-related, and that cooking fires spike on Thanksgiving with an average of 1,400 cooking fires, more than three times the daily average. It says not to leave cooking food unattended, to keep children and pets at least three feet away, to keep oven mitts, wooden utensils, and food packaging away from the stovetop, and to turn pot handles inward. It says that if a grease fire starts, smother the flames by sliding a lid over the pan and turning off the burner, and never to use water. It says to have a fire extinguisher nearby and know how to use it, and to have a working smoke alarm on each level, outside sleeping areas, and inside bedrooms.
  • The NFPA Educational Messages Desk Reference says to stay in the kitchen when frying, boiling, grilling, or broiling, and to turn the burner off if you leave. It says simmering, baking, or roasting is checked regularly, with a timer, while you stay in the home. It says to keep a lid nearby. For a small grease fire in a pan, it says to slide the lid on, turn off the burner, not move the pan, and leave the lid on until the pan has cooled. It says never to pour water on a cooking-pan grease fire. It says only Class K extinguishers are designed for grease and animal-fat fires, and that they should be used by trained adults. For an oven fire, it says to turn off the heat and keep the door closed. It says that when in doubt, get out, close the door, and call 911 from outside.
  • On an extinguisher, read the classification label and look for a UL Listed or ULC Listed mark. USFA’s extinguisher page says Class B is for combustible and flammable liquids such as grease, gasoline, oil, and oil-based paints, and Class K is for vegetable oils, animal oils, and fats in cooking appliances, often in commercial kitchens. It says multipurpose extinguishers labeled B-C or A-B-C can be used on most types of home fires. Read the model number and the date code on the cylinder before comparing a unit with a recall notice. The Kidde notice below names plastic-handle and push-button Pindicator extinguishers, not every extinguisher.

How to search a recall

For a purchase in the United States, search the brand and the model on CPSC Recalls. For a purchase in Canada, search the same words on Health Canada recalls and safety alerts. Open the notice and match the model number on the unit. A family name on the box can point at a different model.

The CPSC Kidde notice below includes a count of units sold in Canada. Search the model number printed on the label on Health Canada recalls and safety alerts as well as on CPSC Recalls. Oil turkey fryers are a separate check on the turkey fryers guide. Space heaters are a separate check on the space heaters guide.

Patterns in official notices

Each pattern below is one official page. The sentence is what that page says.

  • CPSC: Put safety on the menu, November 19, 2025. The release, number 26-093, says the holiday season is the most dangerous time of year for cooking-related fires and injuries. It says 45 percent of annual residential fires are cooking-related, according to CPSC’s report on residential fire and loss estimates. It says cooking fires spike on Thanksgiving, with an average of 1,400 cooking fires, more than three times the daily average. It says never to leave cooking unattended, to keep children and pets at least three feet away, and to smother a grease fire with a lid rather than water. For a turkey fryer it says to follow the manufacturer’s instructions, never to overheat or overfill the oil, and to keep the fryer out of the garage, off the deck, and away from the house.
  • NFPA: Educational Messages Desk Reference: cooking fires and extinguishers. Section 8.4 says to smother a small pan grease fire with a lid, turn off the burner, and not move the pan. It says never to pour water on that fire. It says only Class K extinguishers are designed for grease and animal-fat fires and that they should be used by trained adults. Section 21.4 says PASS means pull the pin, aim low at the base of the fire, squeeze the lever slowly, and sweep the nozzle from side to side. It says a portable extinguisher is for a small fire that is not spreading quickly, when everyone is evacuating, someone outside is calling 911, and there is a clear escape route. Section 21.4.4 says not to use a portable extinguisher on a grease fire unless you are at least 6 to 8 feet away, and not to move a pot of burning grease. It says to cover the pan and shut off the heat.
  • USFA: Choosing and using fire extinguishers. The page, last reviewed April 1, 2023, says to look for a UL Listed or ULC Listed label. It says Class B is for combustible and flammable liquids such as grease, gasoline, oil, and oil-based paints. It says Class K is for vegetable oils, animal oils, and fats in cooking appliances and is often found in commercial kitchens. It says multipurpose extinguishers labeled B-C or A-B-C can be used on most types of home fires. It says to use an extinguisher only when others have been alerted, someone has called the fire department, the person can operate it, the fire is small and contained, the person is safe from toxic smoke, and there is a clear escape route. It says PASS is pull, aim, squeeze, and sweep. It says to check that nothing blocks the extinguisher, that the gauge shows the recommended pressure, and that the can, hose, and nozzle are not damaged, dented, or rusted.
  • CPSC: Kidde recalls fire extinguishers with plastic handles. The notice is dated November 2, 2017. The title includes one death reported. It says the extinguishers can become clogged or require excessive force to discharge and can fail to activate, and that the nozzle can detach with enough force to pose an impact hazard. It covers two styles: plastic-handle extinguishers and push-button Pindicator extinguishers. It says the plastic-handle recall involves 134 models manufactured from January 1, 1973 through August 15, 2017, including models recalled in March 2009 and February 2015, sold in red, white, and silver, and rated ABC or BC. It says the model number is on the label. For units made in 2007 and later, it says a 10-digit date code is on the side of the cylinder near the bottom, and that digits five through nine are the day and year in DDDYY form. It says date codes for recalled models made from January 2, 2012 through August 15, 2017 are 00212 through 22717. It says about 37.8 million were sold in the United States, 2.7 million in Canada, and 6,730 in Mexico. It tells owners to contact Kidde for a replacement. Match the model and date code on the cylinder to the notice. A different model is a different check.

Questions

What do CPSC and NFPA say to do with a small grease fire?

The November 19, 2025 CPSC release says to slide a lid over the pan, turn off the burner, and never use water. The NFPA desk reference says the same, and it says not to move the pan and to leave the lid on until the pan has cooled. It says only Class K extinguishers are designed for grease and animal-fat fires, and that they should be used by trained adults. It also says that when in doubt, get out and call 911 from outside.

What does USFA say to read on a home extinguisher?

USFA says to look for a UL Listed or ULC Listed label. It describes Class B for grease and other flammable liquids, Class K for vegetable oils, animal oils, and fats in cooking appliances, and multipurpose A-B-C or B-C extinguishers for most home fires. It says PASS is pull, aim, squeeze, and sweep, and it lists the questions to answer before using one.

Does the Kidde notice cover every kitchen extinguisher?

No. The November 2, 2017 CPSC notice names plastic-handle models made from January 1, 1973 through August 15, 2017, and push-button Pindicator extinguishers. It says the model number is on the label and describes the date code for units made in 2007 and later. It counts 2.7 million units sold in Canada. Search that model on CPSC Recalls and on Health Canada recalls and safety alerts.
